# Americano

Espresso topped with hot water. This entry describes what the shot usually has to do when water is added after it leaves the portafilter.

## What this type covers

Hot water added to an espresso: an americano, and a long black, which pours the espresso over the water instead of the water over the espresso. A strong cup usually has about two to three parts water to one part espresso, and a milder one more. The espresso itself can be any ratio; the ratio table and the shot styles are in Espresso Brewing Basics.

## What the shot has to do in this cup

Survive dilution. Water thins body and softens flavour, so a shot that tastes fine on its own can taste weak or papery in the cup. What carries through water is clarity and sweetness. Harshness does not: water spreads bitterness and astringency over the whole cup, so a shot with little of either is the goal.

## What it usually means for the shot's numbers

These are tendencies, and the rules and the Recipe's own shots outrank them.

- **Ratio.** A shot with some intensity tends to hold up: around 1:2 or a little shorter is a common starting point. A turbo shot (about 1:2 to 1:3) is lighter in body than a traditional one and tends to give a lighter drink, and an allongé is already long, so it takes less water.
- **Roast and body.** Medium and medium-dark roasts tend to give a round, sweet cup under water. Light roasts keep their fruit when the extraction is clean, but sourness stays sharp when diluted. Very dark roasts tend to turn bitter and ashy.
- **Temperature** follows the roast (Temperature Guidelines by Roast, in Espresso Brewing Basics). If the diluted cup is harsh, the grind comes first, as in the order of adjustment in Espresso Brewing Basics, and the temperature after it.

## What tastes wrong in this cup

- **Watery, weak, empty** usually means the shot was too light for the water. A shorter ratio, a finer grind or a bigger dose adds body back.
- **Sharp, sour, thin** usually points to under-extraction, which dilution makes more obvious: a finer grind or a slightly hotter shot.
- **Bitter, dry, ashy** usually points to over-extraction or a roast that is too dark: a coarser grind or a cooler shot.
- **Muddy or flat with a lot of water** often means more water than the shot can carry. Use less water or a more concentrated shot.

## How the drink notes matter

The water is what the notes are for: how much, how hot, and whether it goes in before or after the shot. Water straight off the boil tends to scald the espresso and add bitterness, and water that is too cool makes a dull, lukewarm cup, so hot but not boiling tends to taste best. Served over ice, the drink dilutes further as the ice melts, which tends to call for a stronger shot.
